# Anivia

Area control and spell cycles

## Playstyle

Shape movement with the wall and storm, then use Frostbite after the correct chilling setup. Prepare space before opponents reach you.

## Strengths

Persistent area control and terrain creation punish narrow approaches.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

Walls can also trap allies. Leave a friendly retreat route and do not assume the egg will survive without protection.

## Before the teamfight

Choose the entrance or area your team wants to hold. Place yourself where you can affect that space without being the first target of an enemy engage.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Place the storm across an approach and use the wall to split the enemy formation. Keep the stun available for a diver if your team lacks protection.

## During the fight

Cast where movement is constrained or an ally has created setup. Keep a defensive response for the enemy who can bypass the zone, then reposition during cooldowns.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Use spells to limit the enemy approach and clear safely. Do not walk into unknown ground to force a full damage sequence when a defensive zone can buy time.

## After the fight

Move the zone forward with your allies only after checking vision. Conserve enough resources for the next contest rather than spending every spell on a retreating target.

## Teamfight positioning

Use the wall to divide enemy movement while leaving allies an exit. Place the storm where opponents must cross or remain; a wall behind your own team can remove their retreat.

## Apply it: a river contest

Hypothetical: both teams approach a river entrance. One enemy moves forward while another remains out of sight. As Anivia, apply this rule before acting: Place the storm across an approach and use the wall to split the enemy formation. Keep the stun available for a diver if your team lacks protection. If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: connect spell use with position

Use the early exchanges to learn where an opponent must move and which spell you need if they cross toward you. Do not spend the full kit on a low-value attempt when doing so leaves no answer to an approach. If you are managing a wave, compare the resources used to clear it with what remains for the next contest. If you are supporting or jungling, apply the same resource check to the next useful route. A spell that misses can still influence movement, but only if you retain a position from which the opponent cannot freely punish the cooldown.

## Midgame: arrive before the space is lost

Choose the entrance or area the team wants to control before enemies settle into it. Move with allies so you can set pressure from protected ground rather than walking in as the first target. If the team arrives late, use safe information and ranged influence where available; do not assume a powerful zone lets you enter unknown terrain alone. Let the frontline or another supported ally create the first stable position. Advance your influence with them. A prepared area is valuable because opponents must pay a cost to cross it, not because you cast every spell the moment an objective appears.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. Your storm covers the river entrance, but an ally is retreating through the same narrow space. Place the wall only after checking which side each teammate will occupy. Splitting two enemies is useful; cutting off your own carry may undo that value. Hold the stun for the enemy who crosses the zone toward you.

## Read movement before selecting the cast

Against a compact formation, place pressure across a route several enemies need. Against spread opponents, focus on the path of the immediate threat or a reachable target rather than chasing maximum area damage. Against a diver, reserve a response and enough space for it to work; delayed control may not stop access immediately. Against enemies who refuse your prepared ground, decide whether holding it already achieves the objective. You do not need to leave a good position simply because nobody walks into the obvious spell. Denying a route can help the team even when the damage numbers are small.

## Think in two spell cycles

Plan where you will stand after the first cast sequence, not only where that sequence lands. Use allied setup or constrained movement to improve the chance of useful contact. While abilities recover, reposition behind support and watch the opponent who can bypass the zone. Avoid stepping forward for a small extra hit if it removes the room needed for your next full sequence. When the fight moves, move your influence with it instead of continuing to cast at the original location. A good first zone can become irrelevant once both teams change direction, so update the plan before spending the next important tool.

## Use control differently when behind

When ahead, restrict the enemy's safe approaches and advance with allies after the relevant threats are checked. When behind, prioritize safe wave pressure, delayed approaches and a clear retreat route. Do not walk into unknown ground to manufacture a full damage sequence against a stronger formation. Let the enemy enter a position where control and allied focus can reduce its advantage. If your main defensive spell misses, give ground early instead of waiting until the opponent reaches you. Recovery depends on preserving another useful cast window, not proving that every lost area can be defended from the same standing position.

## Convert a controlled area into a result

After forcing enemies away, check whether the team can safely use the space for a structure, objective, wave or reset. Preserve resources for the next contest rather than spending everything on an opponent already leaving useful range. In review, examine whether enemies had to cross your threatened area or could simply walk around it. Then inspect the position of your own body while the zone was active. A strong placement may still fail if you stand where an unrelated threat can reach you. Practise changing the angle or timing so both the area and the caster remain useful throughout the exchange.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.
