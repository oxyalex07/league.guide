# Vel'Koz

Ranged pressure before commitment

## Playstyle

Use angled shots and spell combinations to build the passive while maintaining a protected firing position.

## Strengths

Long-range pressure and passive damage reward accurate repeated spell hits.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

An immobile channel is vulnerable to flanks. Stop chasing the ideal beam line when a threat can reach your position.

## Before the teamfight

Find a clear firing line and a safe location to move to after casting. Check flank routes before focusing on distant targets.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Find a clear line through controlled enemies before channeling the ultimate. Keep the knock-up for a diver when allies cannot protect the channel.

## During the fight

Use range to lower enemy health before a hard commitment. If a diver reaches you, switch to survival and repositioning instead of finishing a low-value long-range cast.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Defend a clear line with allies and use safe wave pressure. Do not trade your life for one more spell on a distant opponent who can still return to safety.

## After the fight

Turn the health advantage into controlled space with the team. If enemies leave your range, advance only after allies secure the next approach.

## Teamfight positioning

Fire from protected distance while allies screen the approach. The arrow shows a spell line; keep a retreat route if the enemy closes.

If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: build pressure you can keep

Look for a clear line when the opponent is occupied by movement, an attack or a resource. Cast from a place where you can move after the attempt rather than standing still to watch the result. If the spell misses, keep the defensive response needed for their approach. Balance resource use with the next wave or contest; repeated speculative casts can leave you unable to pressure when enemies finally commit. If your champion also uses attacks, take them only when the closer range is safe. The point of ranged pressure is to improve a later decision, not to land every possible small hit.

## Midgame: establish the firing position first

Move toward the next contest with allies and identify both the firing line and the route away from a flank. Place pressure from controlled ground before the enemy reaches comfortable engage range. If your team arrives late, do not stand alone at an unchecked entrance merely because your spells reach through it. Wait for information or a supported route. Your range is useful only while the casting position survives. If opponents take cover, move with allies to create another angle rather than walking forward until the original range advantage has disappeared and the enemy can start a direct fight.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. A clear beam line appears while a missing enemy could reach your side. Keep the knock-up for that approach and improve your channel position before using the ultimate. Land repeated setup from protection instead of chasing an ideal angle. A slightly shorter safe damage window can be more useful than a full channel attempted from exposed ground.

## Read the enemy answer to pressure

Against a team that must approach through a narrow route, repeated ranged damage can make entry costly. Against strong engage, check who can cross the distance and keep a response for that champion. Against sustain or protection, ask whether the damage is actually changing the upcoming contest rather than assuming every hit creates a lasting advantage. Against a flank, turn attention to survival before finishing another distant cast. The enemy does not need to win the long-range exchange if it can reach your position directly. Track that access separately from the health bar of the opponent you are trying to weaken.

## Turn a health lead into a good commitment

After useful damage lands, tell the difference between pressure and a confirmed opportunity. A lower-health enemy may still hold control, an escape and nearby protection. Let your team use the advantage by taking space or forcing an unfavorable approach rather than chasing the wounded target alone. When allies commit, cast along the movement they constrain and keep your own position behind the fight. If a channel is part of your kit, verify its location before starting and stop when the safety conditions change. A partial safe contribution can preserve another sequence where an uninterrupted but exposed attempt would end your fight.

## Recover when the enemy closes

When the opponent reaches close range, stop following the distant damage plan. Use the available defensive tool, move toward support and establish a new line. If the team is behind, clear or pressure from the area it can actually defend instead of standing at the most aggressive possible range. If ahead, resist using that lead to face-check; unseen access can still remove the advantage. When a key defensive spell is unavailable, give the enemy less space to punish by retreating early. Treat movement between volleys as part of damage output because it is what makes the next volley possible.

## Review the value of each firing line

After the enemy gives ground, move with allies and check the next approach before beginning another sequence. Convert the pressure into a reachable objective, wave or reset rather than spending all resources on a retreating target. In review, record where you stood before each meaningful hit and which threat could have reached that spot. Compare successful pressure with attempts that forced you to retreat immediately or die. The practice goal may be a better angle, a later cast or an earlier step backward. Accuracy matters, but the position that lets you repeat accurate casts often matters more than the longest possible shot.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.

## Replay exercise

Review three teamfights as Vel'Koz. Pause before your first committed move. Name your intended target, the enemy response you needed to respect and your route back. Compare the replay with the champion-specific plan above. Record whether your first move used the strength described here, exposed the weakness, or did both. Choose one change to practise in the next game; judge the decision using the information visible at the time, not the final score.
