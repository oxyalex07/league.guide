# Cho'Gath

Initiation and disruption

## Playstyle

Use the threat of Rupture to control approaches and layer silence after allies restrict movement. Build durable presence through Feast opportunities.

## Strengths

Multiple forms of disruption and growing durability make him hard to ignore.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

Delayed control can miss mobile targets. Follow allied setup or place it on a necessary route instead of always aiming at current position.

## Before the teamfight

Choose the target and the ally who will follow. Check that this ally is in range, has relevant abilities and is not occupied by a different threat.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Stand between the enemy and your damage dealers. Use control on divers, and reserve Feast for a reachable finish or objective decision rather than chasing through the team.

## During the fight

After the first control effect, look back at your damage dealers. Chain control on the original target only while the team can use it; otherwise turn toward the enemy reaching your carry.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Use control to punish an enemy crossing into your team. A defensive catch can be more useful than a long engage that your weaker team cannot finish.

## After the fight

Once the target falls or escapes, stop moving forward until you know where the surviving enemies are. Help the team take safe space rather than beginning a second unsupported dive.

## Teamfight positioning

Start beside allied follow-up. The arrow shows a possible entry toward a reachable enemy, not an instruction to dive every time.

## Apply it: a river contest

Hypothetical: both teams approach a river entrance. One enemy moves forward while another remains out of sight. As Cho'Gath, apply this rule before acting: Stand between the enemy and your damage dealers. Use control on divers, and reserve Feast for a reachable finish or objective decision rather than chasing through the team. If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: threaten without overcommitting

Stand where an opponent must respect a possible start while keeping your ally within useful follow-up distance. A held control spell can deny a route or make a resource difficult to contest even when you never cast it. Watch how the opponent moves when your threat becomes available. If your partner is low, occupied or returning from base, adjust the position instead of playing as if full damage support exists. When moving between areas, check the route before spending your entry tool. Reaching an enemy is only the opening action; the exchange still needs a target, allied damage and a survivable continuation.

## Midgame: prepare the place to fight

Help the team approach the next objective together. Use available information tools and allies to check entrances rather than assuming personal durability makes an unknown route safe. Choose the area where your damage dealers can stand and the path your control should keep enemies from using. If the team is late, look for a defensive catch or a safe trade elsewhere instead of forcing a blind entry into established enemy ground. Preparation reduces the distance between your first control and the follow-up. It also makes retreat possible when the enemy refuses the fight you wanted to start.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. A mobile opponent steps into Rupture range, then pauses with an escape available. Aim at the route they must use or wait for allied setup. Stay between the immediate threat and your damage dealers. Save Feast for a reachable finish rather than abandoning that position to pursue the same opponent through their teammates.

## Choose the target by follow-up

Against a clustered team, several targets may be available but only some are reachable by allied damage. Against a mobile carry, the first action may need allied setup or a constrained escape route. Against a diving team, the most useful engage can be a counter-engage on the opponent who has already entered your formation. Do not measure success only by how many enemies are affected. A single important target held beside your team may decide more than a large group caught beyond its range. Before starting, identify the ally who will use the window and verify their position rather than assuming they will arrive.

## Layer control and check the response

Use the first action to create a shared focus, then watch whether the enemy spends an escape, protection or counter-engage. Add the next control when it provides useful extra time or prevents a specific response; avoid overlapping every available effect without a reason. Once allies begin dealing damage, decide whether to stay on the target or turn toward the threat reaching your backline. Your job can change within the same fight. If the first engage misses, do not automatically spend the second tool chasing the original idea. Rebuild the formation and keep enough control to answer the enemy's attempt to punish the miss.

## Distinguish durability from permission

Surviving the first hit does not mean a deep position is useful. Count the enemies who can continue attacking you and the allies who can punish them for doing so. When ahead, use control to secure a clear conversion rather than forcing a spectacular but unsupported dive. When behind, keep the entry shorter and make enemy approach expensive. Your control remains useful even if you cannot absorb a long response. If allies turn away, treat that as new information and protect their exit where possible. Do not rely on them reversing direction after you have already committed to an isolated target.

## Review the setup, not just the hit

After the fight, help secure the next safe approach and let the team reset if resources are low. Do not begin a second engage because a cooldown has returned while allies are still recovering from the first. In review, pause immediately before commitment and measure the practical gap to allied damage. Then check whether a wall, wave or enemy threat blocked their route. A landed ability can coexist with a poor decision. The useful lesson is often to start later, choose a nearer target or stand where the same control also protects your team if the forward opportunity disappears.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.

## Replay exercise

Review three teamfights as Cho'Gath. Pause before your first committed move. Name your intended target, the enemy response you needed to respect and your route back. Compare the replay with the champion-specific plan above. Record whether your first move used the strength described here, exposed the weakness, or did both. Choose one change to practise in the next game; judge the decision using the information visible at the time, not the final score.
