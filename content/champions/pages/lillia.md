# Lillia

Sustained melee pressure

## Playstyle

Keep movement available through repeated spell hits and use the outer swing to pressure. Work around the edge of melee range.

## Strengths

Movement and multi-target sleep can turn scattered contact into a coordinated fight.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

She must expose herself to apply effects. Do not run through the whole team solely to add one more sleep target.

## Before the teamfight

Name a reachable first target and the enemy spell that could stop your contact. Approach beside allied pressure or through cover rather than absorbing the entire enemy opening alone.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Apply Dream Dust to useful targets before the ultimate. Signal the sleep window so allies can prepare damage instead of taking an uncoordinated early hit.

## During the fight

Keep contact only while your damage, defensive window and allied support overlap. Between spell cycles, move toward your team if the target leaves useful range.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Take shorter contact windows and help stop a diver. Your control and body position can still help even when you cannot survive an extended duel.

## After the fight

Recheck health, resources and the next defensive cooldown. Winning the first exchange does not mean you can safely chase across the next screen.

## Teamfight positioning

Work beside the frontline and keep a route back to allies. The arrow shows contact with a reachable opponent, not a mandatory backline dive.

If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: choose the length of the exchange

Learn what lets you maintain contact and what forces you to leave. A short exchange is useful when your first actions are strong but the enemy response would win a longer fight. A sustained exchange needs a reachable target, resources and enough room to keep moving. Check the surrounding units and nearby enemies before turning contact into a chase. In lane, a large hostile wave can change the result; in the jungle, a second opponent can arrive from an unchecked route. End the exchange when its conditions disappear. Continuing merely because the fight has already begun is not a plan.

## Midgame: connect side pressure and team presence

Decide whether the next useful action is a safe side wave, a nearby skirmish or a grouped contest. Do not choose side pressure without accounting for missing enemies and the time needed to join allies. If the team is already being approached, arriving through a supportable route may be more useful than taking one more resource. If no contest is imminent, gather what you can safely use and prepare the next movement. Your melee presence has value when allies can benefit from the space it creates. Walking ahead of the formation without a specific reason can give opponents a free focus target.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. Dream Dust is active on two useful targets while a third stands deep inside the enemy team. Take the coordinated sleep opportunity already available. Chasing the third mark may expose you to control before your team can act. Signal the timing and prepare follow-up so the sleep becomes shared damage rather than several disconnected attacks.

## Identify the reachable fight

Against a ranged team, look for cover, allied control or a constrained approach that reduces the distance you must cross. Against other melee threats, ask which opponent can be held near your team long enough for repeated damage. Against a protected carry, do not assume the correct job is always to dive. Stopping the enemy diver may provide the same team advantage with much less travel. Choose an accessible first target and update that choice when movement changes the formation. A valuable target is one your kit and team can affect now, not simply the enemy with the largest damage total.

## Use the defensive window while it matters

Plan protection around the response you expect after contact. Different champions use different forms of durability, recovery or avoidance, so follow the specific kit plan above rather than copying another champion's timing. Enter when the defensive window and allied pressure overlap. While it lasts, maintain useful contact instead of spending the whole period chasing. When it ends, move back toward support unless another safe sequence is available. Recovery that depends on hitting something does not help when the target leaves reach. A defensive tool also does not replace an exit from a fight where every enemy can continue attacking you.

## Switch between pressure and protection

If the enemy carry remains unreachable, turn toward the threat your teammates can damage. Use body position and any available control to limit that enemy's access while continuing your own pressure. If allied control creates a clear opening, move forward together and reassess after the first response. When ahead, avoid taking damage from several enemies solely because you can survive the first few hits. When behind, choose shorter contact and clearer follow-up. A useful disruption followed by survival can contribute more than a long solo exchange that looks active but leaves your team without your next spell cycle.

## Review the decision to stay

After every major action, ask whether the target is still reachable, allies can still help and your resources still support another exchange. If one answer changes, choose a new position before chasing. After a win, use your presence to secure safe space for the team's objective rather than beginning another isolated fight. In review, pause when your main defensive window ends and inspect the next movement. Compare leaving then with the continuation you chose. This reveals whether the problem was the original entry or the later decision to remain after the good part of the fight was already over.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.
