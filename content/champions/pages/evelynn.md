# Evelynn

Picks and flank timing

## Playstyle

Approach through unseen space and plan around Allure's setup. Keep an exit angle before revealing yourself.

## Strengths

Camouflage and burst create pressure on isolated enemies and side routes.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

Detection and grouped protection restrict access. Do not force a flank that the enemy has already seen and prepared for.

## Before the teamfight

Find the enemy protection spell that prevents your kill and a route back after the attempt. Approach through checked cover; being unseen is useful only if the landing is safe.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Wait until the enemy's attention is elsewhere, then threaten a vulnerable target. Use the ultimate's backward movement to leave the dangerous part of the fight.

## During the fight

Enter when allied pressure divides attention or a key response is unavailable. After the first damage sequence, leave or change angle rather than remaining exposed while waiting for cooldowns.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Help finish targets already damaged by allies and threaten an edge without forcing entry. Avoid spending the whole fight waiting for a solo kill that your damage cannot secure.

## After the fight

Use the numbers advantage to return to your team and a nearby objective. A second distant target is lower priority if the enemy can collapse on your exit.

## Teamfight positioning

Threaten the side while allies occupy attention. The arrow is a conditional flank; confirm the target, enemy defenses and exit before following it.

## Apply it: a river contest

Hypothetical: both teams approach a river entrance. One enemy moves forward while another remains out of sight. As Evelynn, apply this rule before acting: Wait until the enemy's attention is elsewhere, then threaten a vulnerable target. Use the ultimate's backward movement to leave the dangerous part of the fight. If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: earn a safe opening

Build a position from which a short exchange does not cost your entire health bar or leave you trapped. Watch what the opponent uses to clear the wave or contest a nearby resource. That action can create an opening, but first check whether their partner or jungler can answer. If you are laning, account for the minions you leave when moving toward a side route. If you are jungling, compare a speculative chase with the safe camp or cover opportunity you give up. Pressure is useful when the opponent must respect it; you do not need to spend your full sequence every time they enter reach.

## Midgame: create the pick before the fight

Move after useful information, not merely after the first enemy portrait appears. A target collecting a wave is a possible pick only if you know enough about nearby defenders and can return after the attempt. Approach with an ally or through checked cover while the rest of the team has something useful to do. If nobody can use the pressure, a long flank may leave your own formation outnumbered. When the target retreats early, take the space you earned and regroup. Do not reveal from the same route repeatedly and expect the enemy to stop preparing for it.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. Allure threatens an exposed opponent, but nearby enemies begin moving toward your approach. Change the angle instead of revealing into prepared protection. When allies occupy that response, commit to the vulnerable target with an ultimate exit directed away from the dangerous formation. A hidden approach has value only while the destination remains useful.

## Read the enemy formation

Against grouped protection, wait for the defender to spend attention or an important response on someone else. Against a separated target, verify that the separation is real rather than a short gap in vision. Against a team that engages onto you, a defensive finish beside your own carry may be the best opening. These are different jobs: assassination, edge pressure and counterattack. Choose the one the current formation permits. If the enemy keeps a defensive tool unused because you are nearby, that restraint can help your teammates. Do not throw away that pressure simply because you have not yet dealt damage.

## Run a complete entry and exit sequence

Before moving in, name the first action, the enemy response you expect and the location of your next safe action. During entry, watch the response rather than staring only at the target's health bar. A shield, displacement or newly arriving defender can change the value of the continuation. After using your initial damage, decide whether you can keep contact without losing the exit. Leave when the next safe damage window is worse than returning to allies. A surviving target does not automatically mean the attempt failed: forcing a retreat or an important defensive spell can create the next team advantage.

## Change the plan when the fight turns

When ahead, use the threat of a fast kill to restrict enemy routes, but keep checking whether the opponent has gathered enough protection to punish you. When behind, reduce the demand you place on your own damage: help finish a target already pressured by allies or keep a vulnerable enemy from freely entering. If the first approach is seen, change the timing or route instead of speeding up the same failed entry. If your team retreats, retreat too unless a specific safe action can change the situation. Waiting at a flank while teammates die is not the same as preserving a useful threat.

## Convert pressure into something the team keeps

After a successful pick, identify the safe wave, structure or objective your team can actually reach. Return toward that shared action instead of pursuing a second target into unknown ground. If the target escapes, use the information gained to decide whether another attempt is plausible before your resources recover. In a replay, compare the value of your first entry with the value of your next movement. Many good picks become poor outcomes because the player keeps chasing after the useful fight has ended. A clean exit leaves you available to threaten the next approach and helps your team retain its numbers advantage.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.

## Replay exercise

Review three teamfights as Evelynn. Pause before your first committed move. Name your intended target, the enemy response you needed to respect and your route back. Compare the replay with the champion-specific plan above. Record whether your first move used the strength described here, exposed the weakness, or did both. Choose one change to practise in the next game; judge the decision using the information visible at the time, not the final score.
