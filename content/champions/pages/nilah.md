# Nilah

Dueling and selective entry

## Playstyle

Use close-range attacks and ability hits to sustain pressure, with allied support nearby. Preserve Slipstream paths for repositioning.

## Strengths

Close clustered fights and allied protection support repeated melee-range damage.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

Short reach makes the approach dangerous. Do not use every dash to enter when the enemy can still disengage and attack from range.

## Before the teamfight

Compare side-lane pressure with joining the team. If grouping, identify which enemy control must be used before you can safely maintain close contact.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Join after the initial engage and pull reachable enemies with the ultimate. Use Jubilant Veil against the attack window that would otherwise stop your damage.

## During the fight

Fight a reachable target and preserve the movement or defense needed for the response. Shift deeper only after your team creates a clear advantage.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Avoid an isolated duel that the enemy can easily win. Catch safe resources and group for defensive control or a coordinated finish when side pressure is no longer safe.

## After the fight

Choose between regrouping and a safe side-lane objective. Account for missing enemies before converting a skirmish win into a solo chase.

## Teamfight positioning

Use an edge of the fight while allies occupy the front. The arrow is a conditional entry after enemy control is spent, not permission to split from the team.

If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: establish a repeatable trade

Use short exchanges to learn the opponent's response and your own exit route before demanding an extended duel. Prepare the resource, mark, stack or ability state described in the champion plan. Do not assume that having every button available means the setup is ready. Watch the nearby wave and missing enemies before extending. If you spend a movement or defensive action to begin, know what remains to answer retaliation. A good trade can end without a kill. Preserving enough health and position to contest the next resource is often more useful than forcing one more hit into the opponent's strongest response.

## Midgame: make side pressure a team decision

Choose a side lane only when you can explain what the rest of your team gains while you are there. Check the wave's location, likely enemy answers, retreat routes and the next shared contest. If several enemies disappear, reduce the depth of the push until information returns. If allies cannot avoid a fight, decide whether your pressure can produce a real trade or whether you must join. Do not split simply because the champion is associated with dueling. The value comes from creating a difficult enemy decision while your team remains able to use the resulting space or numbers.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. Allied protection is ready, but the opponent still has room to disengage from your approach. Keep a movement option for the continuation instead of spending every dash to arrive. Enter when allies can sustain the close fight and use the ultimate where their damage can follow. Recheck the formation if enemies leave your effective range.

## Choose a grouped role before arriving

In a teamfight, a clean isolated duel may not exist. Decide whether you will pressure a side angle, follow allied control or protect a teammate against the first diver. Against several ready disables, wait for the response that would stop your damage to be spent or occupied. Against enemies who spread, look for a target whose escape you can contest without leaving support entirely. Against a compact group, accept a reachable frontline exchange. Your best opponent in a side lane may not be the best first target in a crowded fight where every movement invites several additional responses.

## Preserve one answer after the opening

Before beginning a sequence, identify the tool or route you will use when the opponent reacts. It may be movement, a timed defense, terrain or an allied position; use the actual kit rather than assuming a universal escape. Do not spend every option to reach a target who still holds their important response. Once contact begins, alternate damage with position checks. A precision mechanic only helps when the place required to execute it is survivable. Give up the perfect angle when reaching it would put you inside several enemies. The next safe exchange is usually worth more than completing one pattern at any cost.

## Adjust the commitment to the game state

When ahead, make the opponent answer pressure without offering an easy collapse. The lead helps you win some exchanges, but it does not reveal missing enemies or remove control. When behind, gather safe resources and look for coordinated finishes or defensive contributions instead of repeating a lost isolated duel. If the target moves beyond the useful fight, stop and consider what the team needs next. Your ability to chase does not make every chase valuable. Re-enter only when the conditions that support the damage pattern return, rather than relying on the same sequence to produce a different result against stronger protection.

## End the duel and choose the next job

After a favorable exchange, decide between a safe structure, a wave, a reset and joining allies. Check the map before treating a single opponent's retreat as permission to advance indefinitely. In a grouped fight, move back into shared space when the first target leaves useful range. Review the moment when your available exit became unavailable: did you spend it to begin, use it for minor damage or lose it by walking too far? That distinction gives a concrete practice goal. Fix the earliest decision that removed choice, rather than focusing only on the final hit that killed you.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

## Check your understanding

Answer these questions before rereading the guide. What condition makes your champion's main strength useful? Which enemy response would make you delay the opening? What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. A useful answer names a target, a threat and a next position; “deal more damage” is too vague to guide an action. If your answer depends on an ability interaction you are unsure about, verify it in the current client before building a match plan around it. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.
