# Galio

Protection and counter-engage

## Playstyle

Use wave pressure to create chances to support nearby allies. Threaten taunt when enemies must enter your space.

## Strengths

Area disruption and an allied entry point help answer enemy commitment.

## Weaknesses and how to respond

An ultimate destination is tied to the ally's situation. Do not arrive at a lost position without checking who can follow.

## Before the teamfight

Identify which ally is most able to keep dealing damage and which enemy can reach them. Position within support range without standing in the same exposed line.

## Your champion-specific teamfight plan

Choose an allied landing anchor who will still be useful when Hero's Entrance arrives. After landing, protect your damage dealers or chain control on a reachable group.

## During the fight

Use your response when the threatening action begins, then move with the protected ally. Watch for a second attacker instead of spending every tool on the first one.

## When behind or unable to reach your target

Protect the ally with a real escape route. Use control and shields to leave bad ground together; saving one teammate does not require walking into an unwinnable follow-up.

## After the fight

Stay with the allies who remain vulnerable. Check health and available defensive spells before escorting them through an unwarded route.

After landing, protect your damage dealers or chain control on a reachable group. If the missing enemy could interrupt that plan, keep your current safe position and wait for information or allied setup.

## Early game: make support range useful

Stay close enough to affect the next meaningful exchange without standing in the same exposed line as the ally you help. Identify which enemy action forces your teammate to stop attacking or retreat. Keep a response for that action rather than using every tool on harmless pressure. If you leave to ward or roam, first check whether the ally can safely hold their position and whether the route itself is controlled. Protection includes choosing when to be present. A spell that is ready but out of range cannot help, and following an ally into an unwinnable position may simply give the enemy two targets.

## Midgame: choose who can use your help

Identify the ally who can contribute if kept alive and in position during the next fight. This may change with health, resources, available spells and the shape of the terrain. Do not follow the same teammate automatically through every situation. Move with the part of the formation that has a real next action, and check entrances with support rather than walking alone into unknown ground. If a teammate is isolated beyond rescue, protect the remaining group and look for a safe way to recover space. A successful save preserves a useful continuation; preventing one immediate death is not always enough.

## Worked decision: when the first plan changes

Hypothetical practice situation. An ally is being attacked in river and offers a Hero's Entrance destination. Check whether they can remain in useful ground and whether other teammates can join. If the landing would isolate you both, support the retreat by another route. On a good arrival, control the immediate threat before pursuing a distant opponent.

## Read the order of enemy threats

Against a single diver, preserve the tool that interrupts or weakens their decisive action. Against two threats, decide which one needs your strongest response and which can be handled through movement or allied help. Against ranged pressure, keep the team spread enough to avoid taking the same hit while remaining in support range. Against a team that retreats, do not spend every defensive tool chasing unless you have checked that counter-engage is unavailable. The visible enemy is not always the important one. Scan the route of the missing threat before focusing only on the health bar of the ally directly in front of you.

## Time the response and move with it

Use the champion-specific protection at the point where it changes the outcome: before lethal damage when preparation is required, during a relevant approach when control can deny it, or as the ally needs a new position. Do not follow a fixed button order regardless of what the enemy does. After the response, move with the protected teammate so the next spell remains in range. If they retreat, help preserve that route; if they can continue safe damage, hold the formation. Watch the second attacker while the first is controlled. Spending everything at once can leave no answer when the enemy staggers its commitment.

## When a save becomes too costly

Ask what position you and the ally will occupy after the save. If neither of you can leave and the team cannot follow, a deep rescue attempt may make the loss larger. Use the available range, control and terrain to improve the situation without blindly copying the ally's path. When ahead, preserve the damage source that can finish the useful fight rather than joining an unnecessary chase. When behind, help the team leave bad ground together and retain enough health for the next defense. Accept that protection has limits: an ally still needs a viable action after your tool has been spent.

## Review whether support preserved action

After contact ends, escort vulnerable allies through checked routes and help establish the next safe position. Do not let a successful save turn into an immediate second face-check. In review, record what the protected ally could do after your intervention: attack, retreat, cast or merely delay the same unavoidable death. Then inspect your own position and the tool held for the next threat. This gives a more useful measure than raw healing or shielding totals alone. Good protection changes the team's options, and often the improvement is a small movement before the enemy commits rather than a dramatic reaction after it does.

## Build a personal matchup plan

Before the first meaningful exchange, identify three things about the opponent: how they begin contact, what makes their damage dangerous and how they leave. Compare those actions with your own kit plan instead of labeling the matchup simply good or bad. Pick one observable opening, such as an important response being used or an ally creating setup. Also name one condition that cancels the attempt, such as another enemy arriving or your escape route closing. Use the opening only while the conditions remain true. After a lost exchange, change one decision rather than assuming the entire matchup is impossible. This guide teaches that decision process; it does not provide a numerical matchup ranking or a fixed build for every patch.

## A focused practice session

Begin in practice mode by checking the current tooltips and rehearsing the key sequence without an opponent. Add movement before trying to perform it quickly. Next, practise the same sequence with a deliberate stop: miss the setup, change the target or move away before the continuation. This teaches the choice to cancel rather than only the choice to commit. In a normal game, focus on one measurable behavior for three relevant situations. Record whether you checked the threat and exit before acting. A win does not automatically mean the behavior was correct, and a death does not automatically mean the opening was wrong. Keep the practice target narrow enough that you can describe the next change in one sentence.

## Review without rewriting what you knew

Pause a replay just before your first committed move and use only the information visible to your team at that moment. List visible enemies, missing threats, nearby allies and the important resources you actually had. Predict the next action before continuing playback. Then compare the prediction with the response: was the error an inaccurate assumption, a missed mechanic or a decision to continue after conditions changed? Review one successful exchange as well as one failure so you can repeat the good setup. Do not use later knowledge of a hidden opponent to pretend their exact location was obvious. The useful improvement is a better check, safer route or clearer stopping condition for the next similar situation.

What would you do if your intended target moved out of safe reach? Where should you stand when the first sequence ends? Compare your answers with the playstyle, weakness and worked decision sections. Repeat the exercise after practice and explain which decision you changed and why.
